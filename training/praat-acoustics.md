## 1. Install Praat; open a sound + TextGrid; navigate intervals

1. Install **Praat** (free, from the Praat website; it is a single application with no
   installer to speak of). **The lab pins no Praat version — use a current one** (R6, PI
   2026-08-30). Menu paths do shift between releases, so if a path in this module does not
   match what you see, you are probably on a different version rather than lost: **say so** and
   the module gets fixed.
2. Praat has **two windows**: the **Objects** window (a list of things you have loaded) and
   the **Picture** window (drawing). You will live in Objects.
3. `Open > Read from file...` a `.wav`, then the matching `.TextGrid`. **Select both in the
   Objects list** (click one, ctrl/cmd-click the other) and click **View & Edit**. Selecting
   only the sound gives you a waveform with no annotation — the most common first-day
   confusion.
4. The editor shows the waveform, the spectrogram, and each TextGrid tier as a row of
   labelled intervals. **Tab** plays the visible part; clicking an interval selects it;
   the numbers under the window are the selection's start, end, and duration.

**What an aligned TextGrid looks like** (this is Module 3's subject, in one paragraph): a
`words` tier and a `phones` tier, where phone labels are ARPABET with a stress digit —
`AA1`, `IH0`, `EY2` — and `sil`/`sp` mark silence. The repo's own reader,
`projects/p-oh-lowering/analysis/replication/scripts/lib/textgrid_io.py`, expects exactly
that: tier names `phones` and `words`, and labels matching `^([A-Z]{2,3})([0-2])$`.

## 2. Reading a spectrogram; identifying vowel steady states

The spectrogram plots **time × frequency**, with darkness as energy. Formants are the dark
horizontal bands — resonances of the vocal tract. **F1 and F2 are what carry vowel
identity**, so they are what we measure.

The one intuition worth internalizing:

- **F1 tracks vowel height, inversely.** High vowels (FLEECE, GOOSE) have **low F1**; low
  vowels (TRAP, LOT) have **high F1**.
- **F2 tracks frontness.** Front vowels (FLEECE, DRESS) have **high F2**; back vowels
  (GOOSE, THOUGHT) have **low F2**.

That is why vowel plots put **F2 on a reversed x-axis and F1 on a reversed y-axis** — the
plot then looks like the mouth.
It is also your first QC
instrument: *a high front vowel with a high F1 is not a high front vowel — it is a
measurement error.* You will use exactly that reasoning in Exercise 2.

**Finding the steady state.** A vowel's edges are contaminated by the consonants around it:
formants bend into and out of neighbouring stops and nasals. The measurable part is the
**steady middle**, where the tracks are flattest. Look for the interval where F1 and F2 stop
moving, and measure there — not at the boundary.

**What the repo documents about where to measure.** `p-oh-lowering`'s superseded
Parselmouth path trimmed each vowel to its **middle 20–80%** and took the **median across
analysis frames** (`config.yaml`: `trim_start: 0.20`, `trim_end: 0.80`; the median is in
`scripts/lib/formants.py`). Its live path instead uses FastTrack's smoothed-track sampling.
For **dynamic** vowels, trajectory measures (rather than a single point) may apply. Lab-internal
provenance for these defaults is tracked in the project records.

**Where the measurement-point convention comes from: `new-fave`, unless your project says
otherwise** (R3, PI 2026-08-30). The convention is **project-specific with new-fave as the
default**: use new-fave's unless your project has decided something else, and **confirm which
applies to yours before you measure** (§0). new-fave's documentation is —
[new-fave documentation](https://forced-alignment-and-vowel-extraction.github.io/new-fave/)
`https://forced-alignment-and-vowel-extraction.github.io/new-fave/`. Four things about it are
worth knowing before you measure anything by hand:

- It is built on the **FastTrack** method and is oriented toward formant **tracks**, though it
  can also produce single-point measurements.
- Its measurement-point heuristics come from its **`fave-measurement-point`** dependency and are
  **configurable** — the convention is a setting, not a constant, which is exactly why "which
  point?" is a project question with a sensible default rather than a universal answer.
- It **does not require speaker demographics.** A demographics file is optional and does not
  influence processing.
- It **does not include a forced aligner**, and recommends **MFA** — the aligner Module 3
  (`forced-alignment-mfa.md`) teaches.

**Two workflows, and this module teaches the first.** *Hand-measurement in Praat* uses a **fixed
ceiling chosen for the speaker** (§3.1) and a **measurement point you choose and can defend**.
*Production extraction with new-fave* **sweeps ceilings** via FastTrack and takes its measurement
point from the configured heuristic — which is part of why it needs no demographics. So the fixed
5,000/5,500 Hz ceilings below are the right way to measure by hand and **are not what the
pipeline does**; do not carry the fixed number into a pipeline discussion, or the sweep into your
hand measurements.

## 3. Measuring F1/F2 — settings, and the pitfalls

In the editor: **Formant > Show formants** overlays the tracker's estimates as dotted red
tracks. **Formant > Formant settings...** is where the tracker is configured, and
**Formant > Formant listing** prints values for the current selection.

### 3.1 The formant ceiling is the setting that matters

The **maximum formant (ceiling)** tells Praat the top of the range in which to look for a
fixed number of formants. Set it too low and the tracker crams formants together; set it too
high and it finds spurious ones and starts assigning F3 to the F2 slot. **It is the single
most consequential setting you will touch**, because it interacts with vocal-tract length:
speakers with shorter vocal tracts have higher formants and need a higher ceiling.

**The values to use** — **PI-ratified, 2026-08-30 (R1)**. They are **Praat's own documented
averages**, from the `Sound: To Formant (burg)...` manual page, where **5,500 Hz** is also
Praat's standard default:
[Praat manual — Sound: To Formant (burg)...](https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/praat/manual/Sound__To_Formant__burg____.html)
`https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/praat/manual/Sound__To_Formant__burg____.html`

| | Ceiling |
|---|---|
| Adult male-range speaker | **5,000 Hz** |
| Adult female-range speaker | **5,500 Hz** |

### 3.2 The cautionary tale — a tracking setting can flip a sign

A formant-tracking setting can determine the **sign** of a reported result — and even a claim
that important, made by your own lab, is **testimony until a committed artifact backs it up**.
Lab-internal provenance for the worked case behind both halves of that lesson is tracked in the
project records.


### 3.3 The four ways a tracker goes wrong

1. **It skips a formant.** What it reports as F2 is really F3 (and its F3 is really F4).
   Tell: F2 jumps far above the speaker's range for that vowel while **F1 stays normal**.
2. **It loses F1** — typically a ceiling problem, or low-amplitude/creaky voice. Tell: an
   impossible F1, e.g. a high front vowel with F1 near 700 Hz.
3. **There is not enough vowel to measure.** Very short tokens give the analysis window
   nothing stable. `p-oh-lowering` excludes tokens below **0.05 s** and above **0.5 s**
   (`config.yaml`: `min_duration_sec`, `max_duration_sec`). **Those bounds are that project's,
   not a lab standard** (R4, PI 2026-08-30) — the fourth kind of number in §0 — so check with
   the PI which bounds your own project uses. Some kind of duration floor is normal practice;
   the specific pair is not automatically yours.
4. **Context, misread as error.** Nasalization, laterals, and rhotics genuinely move
   formants — a pre-nasal /æ/ really is higher and fronter. That is the vowel system doing
   something, not the tracker failing. **Never silently drop it.** Code the context and let
   the analysis handle it.

Pitfall 4 is the one that separates a useful RA from a destructive one. Dropping real
variation because it looked odd is how you delete your own result.

## 4. Building a hand-measured validation set

This is your actual deliverable on a project (see a Community-cluster work package). The
shape of it:

1. **Sample, don't cherry-pick.** Take a *random* subset — 20–30 tokens across speakers and
   vowel classes. Measuring the ones that look wrong tells you nothing about the rate.
2. **Measure blind** to the automated value where you can, so you are not anchored.
3. **Log every token**, agreements included, in `measurement-log.csv`. A validation set with
   only disagreements in it is not a validation set.
4. **Compare against a stated tolerance**, agreed in advance with the PI/methodologist.
5. **Investigate disagreements; do not silently exclude them.** Every exclusion gets a
   logged, criterion-based reason.
6. **Never hand-edit the machine-written output table.** If the pipeline is wrong, the
   pipeline gets fixed and re-run. A hand-corrected output file is an untraceable record —
   the lab treats that as worse than the error it fixes.

## 5. Pointer — parselmouth, for the same measurements in Python

`parselmouth` runs Praat's own analysis engine from Python, which is how the lab scripts it.
The repo's worked example is
`projects/p-oh-lowering/analysis/replication/scripts/lib/formants.py`: it extracts the
interval, calls `to_formant_burg(...)`, samples F1/F2 at each frame inside the trimmed

At **3,000 Hz** the tracker still has to place five formants, now inside a band that holds
three real ones — F1, F2 and, at 2,800 Hz, F3 *("two" corrected 2026-09-02, re-check m-2)* — so
F1 mostly survives (TRAP's does not) and the **F2 slot is filled by a spurious resonance at
630–830 Hz**; the true F2 is pushed *up* the list into the F3 or F4 slot. The
reported F2 is far too **low**. At **8,000 Hz** five formants have to cover roughly eight real
resonances, so the tracker **merges neighbours**: the reported F2 lands **above** the true value,
and for FLEECE and LOT F1 is lost outright (Praat's "F1" is 2,530 and 1,015 Hz). The general
statement is what counts — *the ceiling determines which resonance lands in which slot* — and
the table shows why the direction of the error is something to measure, not to predict from an
armchair. Route A (the VowelEditor) synthesizes differently, so expect the same kinds of failure
but not these exact numbers. The same hedge applies across Praat versions: the table was measured
under Praat 6.1.38, the lab pins no version (R6), and a current Praat may place the spurious
formants elsewhere — the *kind* of failure is what to report, and it is what the competence check
grades *(added 2026-09-02, re-check m-6)*.

**Why the tolerances differ, which is the point of the exercise.** They are not a measure of how
carefully you worked. **They are a property of the acoustic event.** A stop burst is a
discontinuity a few milliseconds wide, so ±5 ms is fair and ±40 ms would be sloppy. A vowel
gliding into a nasal murmur has no instant at which one becomes the other — formant amplitudes
fall and the murmur's low resonance rises across tens of milliseconds — so demanding ±5 ms there
would be demanding a precision the signal does not contain, and would reward guessing over
judgement. **Match your claimed precision to the event.** When a boundary is genuinely gradual,
the professional answer is a window and a note, not a confident single number — and if a project
needs one number anyway, the convention for choosing it is a decision to record, not one to
improvise per file.

**What these fixtures cannot teach you** *(added 2026-09-02, review M7)*. Until 2026-09-02 every
join in these files faded to an exact digital zero, so every boundary was an amplitude notch —
"find the dip" would have scored perfectly here and failed on the first real recording. The
joins are now short crossfades, and the verifier checks that no run of zeros exists at any of
them (report §4). But synthetic boundaries are still cleaner than any speech: no coarticulation,
no breath, no room. A real segment boundary is rarely an amplitude minimum; it is where the
*spectrum* changes — periodicity starts or stops, a formant pattern gives way to noise or to a
murmur. Mark from the spectrogram and from the waveform's *character*, not from its envelope.
The tolerances here are fair for these files; on real data they are a floor for your claimed
precision, not a ceiling.
