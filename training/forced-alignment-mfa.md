# Module 3 — Forced alignment (Montreal Forced Aligner)

**Status: DRAFT (2026-08-05)** — content complete; awaiting PI review to flip to READY.

**Purpose.** Understand what forced alignment produces and how to **QC** it. For
analysis-only RAs (path (a)): *concept + a small run* so they can trust and spot-check the
aligned derivatives. For collection/extension RAs (path (b)): this becomes hands-on and
load-bearing.

The reason this module exists at all: **the alignment is upstream of every number the lab
publishes about vowels.** If an interval is in the wrong place, the formant measured inside
it is wrong, the speaker's mean is wrong, and the model is fitted to it anyway without
complaint. Nothing downstream will tell you. Only a person looking will.

**~Time to complete:** 3–5 hours (concept + the QC exercises); more under path (b).

> **Data rule (from Module 0):** every exercise here uses **synthetic practice data only.**
> The practice materials are in `people/training/practice-data/mfa/` and are generated by a
> script. Raw audio and un-anonymized transcripts never enter this repo.

TextGrid and read a spectrogram before misalignment means anything to you.

## 1. What alignment does

**Forced alignment takes audio plus a transcript you already have, and returns time stamps.**
It does not do speech recognition — it is not guessing *what* was said. You tell it what was
said; it works out *when*.

```
  audio (.wav)  +  transcript (.lab / .TextGrid)   ──►   aligner   ──►   TextGrid
                                                            │
                                                pronunciation dictionary
                                                     acoustic model
```

Two resources do the work, and the distinction matters for QC:

- The **pronunciation dictionary** maps each orthographic word to a phone sequence
  (`league → L IY1 G`). A word that is **not in the dictionary** has no pronunciation, so the
  aligner cannot align it. It is reported as **OOV** (out of vocabulary) and typically
  aligned as `spn` ("spoken noise") — a black box that can swallow a large stretch of speech.
  **OOVs are the number-one cause of local alignment damage, and they are the easiest to fix.**
- The **acoustic model** is a trained model of what those phones sound like. It is what makes
  the boundaries land where they do.

**The output** is a TextGrid with (at least) a `words` tier and a `phones` tier. Phone labels
are ARPABET with a stress digit on vowels — `AA1`, `IH0`, `EY2` — and `sil`/`sp` mark
silence. Every phone nests inside its word; every word nests in the file. This is exactly the
structure the lab's own reader expects
(`projects/p-oh-lowering/analysis/replication/scripts/lib/textgrid_io.py`).

**What alignment is not.** It is not correct by construction. It is a model's best fit of a
transcript to a signal, and when the transcript and the signal disagree — a mis-transcribed
word, an OOV, background noise, overlapping speech — the aligner does not stop. It produces
a confident, well-formed, wrong TextGrid.

## 3. Reading the output

Open `practice-data/mfa/practice-aligned.TextGrid` — in Praat if you have it (Module 2 §1),
or with the shipped viewer if you do not:

```bash
cd people/training/practice-data/mfa
python3 inspect_textgrid.py practice-aligned.TextGrid
python3 inspect_textgrid.py practice-aligned.TextGrid --tier phones --min-dur 0.3
```

The viewer prints intervals and durations and **makes no judgement** — it sorts the haystack;
finding the needle is your job.

What to look at first, every time:

- **Do the tiers span the same time?** Both should start at 0 and end at the file's end.
- **Do the words on the words tier match the transcript?** If the aligner stopped partway,
  the tail of the transcript simply is not there.
- **Is there an OOV report, and is it empty?** A non-empty OOV list tells you where to look
  before you look anywhere else.
- **Sort the phones by duration.** This one habit finds most gross failures in seconds.

## 4. QC: spotting misalignment, and re-run vs. hand-correct

### 4.1 What misalignment looks like without audio

You cannot always listen. These signatures are visible in the TextGrid alone:

| Signature | Usual cause |
|---|---|
| A **single very long phone**, especially `spn` | An **OOV word** the aligner could not pronounce; it absorbs the surrounding speech |
| A **stressed vowel far longer than any plausible vowel** | The aligner lost the thread and over-assigned time |
| A **word crushed to near-zero duration** | The compensating half of the failure above |
| **Words missing** relative to the transcript | Alignment stopped partway, or the transcript and audio are misaligned wholesale |
| **Very few words per minute** across a whole file | A flawed or incomplete alignment run — see §4.3 |

**The compensation principle, and it is the thing to actually learn.** A forced aligner is
fitting a *fixed* sequence of phones to a *fixed* stretch of time. Time it wrongly gives to
one segment must come out of a neighbour. **So misalignments come in adjacent compensating
pairs — an implausibly long interval next to an implausibly short one.** When you find one,
look immediately on both sides. Two symptoms are usually one error, and reporting them as two
independent problems means you have not understood the failure.

### 4.2 Re-run or hand-correct?

**Default: re-run.** Fix the *cause* — add the word to the dictionary, correct the
transcript, re-segment the utterance — and align again.

**Hand-correcting boundaries is a last resort**, and it carries a real cost: a hand-edited
TextGrid is no longer reproducible from its inputs, so nobody downstream can regenerate it.
It also runs straight into the lab norm **"never hand-edit a machine-written file"**
(`CLAUDE.md`): if a script wrote it, a script corrects it. If you ever do hand-correct, that
is a **documented, logged decision** with the PI, not a quiet fix.

**Exclusion is a legitimate third option.** A stretch that will not align cleanly can be
excluded — with a logged, criterion-based reason, exactly as for formant tokens (Module 2 §4).

### 4.3 The lab's own cautionary case

`p-oh-lowering` inherited an alignment and checked it. The corpus shipped an
`MFA_Aligned_TextGrids/` directory; **it was rejected and the corpus was re-aligned from the
verbatim transcripts.** The diagnosis, confirmed across all 38 speakers, was that the
pre-existing words tier carried only **1.8 to 8.2 words per minute** and **stopped partway
through each interview**. Two worked cases, *with invented illustrative numbers of the same
order of magnitude — the real per-speaker figures live in the memo, not here (replaced
2026-08-21 during the pre-publication release review)*: **speaker A, ~450 words in the verbatim transcript versus ~230 in
the shipped tier; speaker B, ~1,000 versus ~460.** The payoff for re-aligning was not marginal — the usable token count rose from
**about 421 to 6,114**. Source:
`projects/p-oh-lowering/methods/extraction-and-measurement.md` §1.1.

Two more lessons from the same memo, both worth having in advance:

- **Segmentation matters.** A first attempt flattened each speaker's ~4,000-word transcript
  into a single utterance against a 60–90 minute file. It **failed outright for 3 of 38
  speakers**, each stalling the decoder for 80–108 seconds with no output. Re-using the
  transcript's own per-interval boundaries fixed it: a bad stretch then costs one interval
  rather than an entire interview (§1.2).
- **Transcription conventions must be stripped before alignment.** ELAN markup (cut-offs,
  emphasis, punctuation) is not in the dictionary, so leaving it in **inflates the OOV rate**
  and damages alignment. That project strips it by regex and passes interval timings through
  untouched (§1.2).

**The transferable habit: never assume a shipped alignment is good. Measure its coverage
before you build on it.** That is Exercise 3.

## 5. How aligned intervals feed vowel extraction — the link to Module 2

This is where the two modules join, and it is the whole reason you learned both.

1. The aligner produces phone intervals: `AA1` from 4.950 s to 5.660 s.
2. An extraction script walks the phones tier, keeps intervals whose label is a vowel with
   the wanted stress, and records the surrounding phones as context. The repo's worked
   example is `textgrid_io.py`'s `iter_vowel_tokens(...)`, which yields exactly that —
   vowel, stress, word, `tmin`, `tmax`, preceding and following phone.
3. For each token, a formant measurement is taken **inside that interval** — trimmed away
   from the edges to avoid consonantal transitions, then summarized (Module 2 §2–3;
   `lib/formants.py`).
4. Those measurements are normalized and modelled.

**So a bad boundary is a bad formant, silently.** If the interval labelled `AA1` actually
covers the preceding /w/ and half the vowel, the tracker measures that faithfully and returns
a number that is wrong in a way no downstream QC catches. This is why the duration filters in
Module 2 §3.3 exist: **a wildly long or wildly short vowel is usually an alignment failure
wearing a formant's clothing.** They are the seam between the two modules, and they are your
cheapest detector.

## Answer key

**Ex. 1.** (a) **18 words** in the transcript; **18 labelled word intervals** (of 22 total —
the other 4 are unlabelled). They match, which is the first thing you check and the thing
that fails in §4.3's real case. (b) Unlabelled word intervals are **silence and pauses**;
the phones tier carries `sil` (file-initial/final) or `sp` (a short pause between words) at
those times. (c) The OOV entry is **`iggles`**. Its phones tier shows a **single `spn`
interval, 1.400–2.950 s (1.550 s long)** — one "spoken noise" box instead of a phone
sequence. (d) The word is **not in the pronunciation dictionary**, so the aligner has no
phone sequence to fit and cannot place internal boundaries; it brackets the region as
unknown, absorbing neighbouring speech.

**Ex. 2.** Three defects, **two root causes** — that second number is the point of the
exercise.

| # | Where | What | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D1** | phones **[15]**, 1.400–2.950 s (**1.550 s**), label `spn`; words **[7]** `IGGLES`, same span | A single interval far longer than any phone | **Root cause 1 — OOV.** `iggles` is not in the dictionary (it is in `oov-report.txt`), so it aligns as `spn` and swallows ~1.5 s. **Fix: add the pronunciation or correct the transcript, then re-align.** |
| **D2** | phones **[41]**, 4.950–5.660 s (**0.710 s**), label `AA1`, inside words **[17]** `WATCHED` | A stressed vowel of 0.71 s in running speech — implausible, and above the 0.5 s upper bound `p-oh-lowering` applies | **Root cause 2 —** the aligner over-assigned time to this nucleus. |
| **D3** | words **[18]**, 5.785–5.803 s (**0.018 s**), `THE`; phones **[44]** `DH` and **[45]** `AH0`, 0.009 s each | An entire word compressed to 18 ms | **Same root cause as D2.** The time D2 took had to come from somewhere, and it came from the immediately following word. |

**How many errors? Two.** D2 and D3 are **one misalignment event with two symptoms** —
adjacent, opposite-signed, and directly compensating (§4.1). An answer listing "three
independent problems" has found the symptoms without understanding the failure; an answer
that pairs D2 with D3 and reports the region 4.890–5.803 s as one bad stretch has understood
it. Full credit requires naming D1 separately, since it has a genuinely different cause and a
genuinely different fix.

**Ex. 3.** (a)/(b) **PX03, PX07, and PX11 are unusable.** All others are healthy.

| | words/min | coverage |
|---|---|---|
| **PX03** | **5.6** | **39.2%** |
| **PX07** | **1.8** | **36.3%** |
| **PX11** | **8.2** | **36.7%** |
| the other nine | **116.7 – 141.8** | **99.5 – 99.8%** |

The separation is not subtle — an order of magnitude on rate, and a third of the file versus
essentially all of it. Conversational speech runs on the order of ~120–160 words/min, so a
**red-flag threshold anywhere in the 30–60 words/min range** separates these cleanly.
*(Threshold is a starting default — `ASSUMPTION`, `[confirm]` with the PI. The 1.8–8.2
words/min band the bad files fall in is the range documented for the real rejected PREP
alignment, `p-oh-lowering/methods/extraction-and-measurement.md` §1.1.)*

(c) **Two symptoms, and you should report both**: the alignment is **sparse** (very few words
per minute *where it did run*) **and truncated** (it stops around 36–39% of the way through
the file). Either alone would be a problem; together they say the run failed rather than that
the speakers were quiet.

(d) **Re-run — and re-run all three from the verbatim transcripts, not just patch them.**
Hand-correction is not on the table: you cannot hand-correct words that were never aligned,
and at ~36% coverage the missing material is most of the file. This is the §4.3 case
exactly, where re-aligning took the usable token count from ~421 to 6,114. Before re-running,
check *why* — OOV rate, transcript conventions left in, or a segmentation problem (§4.3).
