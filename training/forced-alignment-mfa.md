# Module 3 — Forced alignment (Montreal Forced Aligner)

**Status: READY** — PI-verified 2026-08-30. *(READY describes this material, not you: you still pass the competence check at the bottom before you run alignment on project data.)*

**Purpose.** Understand what forced alignment produces and how to **QC** it. For
analysis-only RAs (path (a)): *concept + a small run* so they can trust and spot-check the
aligned derivatives. For collection/extension RAs (path (b)): this becomes hands-on and
load-bearing.

The reason this module exists at all: **the alignment is upstream of every number the lab
publishes about vowels.** If an interval is in the wrong place, the formant measured inside
it is wrong, the speaker's mean is wrong, and the model is fitted to it anyway without
complaint. Nothing downstream will tell you. Only a person looking will.

**~Time to complete:** **4–6 hours of work** — reading §§1–6 (about 1–1.5 h) plus the five
exercises: the input/output contract, the seeded-misalignment hunt, the coverage table, your own
end-to-end alignment, and the analysis-table triage. **Plus a one-off 15–45 minutes to build the
MFA environment** for Exercise 4 (§2 Step 1) — that part is mostly **waiting on downloads**
rather than working, it varies with your network, and **if you already have a working MFA
install you skip it entirely.** More under path (b).

> **Data rule (from Module 0):** the exercises here use **synthetic practice fixtures** —
> in `people/training/practice-data/mfa/`, generated by a script — and, for Exercise 4, **a
> recording you make of your own voice.** Never a participant recording, and never a real
> transcript. Raw audio and un-anonymized transcripts never enter this repo; keep your own
> practice recording outside it too.

**Prerequisite:** **Module 2 (`praat-acoustics.md`)** — you need to be able to open a
TextGrid and read a spectrogram before misalignment means anything to you.

## 1. What alignment does

**Forced alignment takes audio plus a transcript you already have, and returns time stamps.**
It does not do speech recognition — it is not guessing *what* was said. You tell it what was
said; it works out *when*.

**The tool.** The lab uses the **Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA)**. Its own documentation is the
reference for anything in this module that is about the software rather than about the lab:

- MFA documentation — [montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io](https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/)
  `https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/`
- Source and issue tracker — [MontrealCorpusTools/Montreal-Forced-Aligner](https://github.com/MontrealCorpusTools/Montreal-Forced-Aligner)
  `https://github.com/MontrealCorpusTools/Montreal-Forced-Aligner`

```
  audio (.wav)  +  transcript (.lab / .TextGrid)   ──►   aligner   ──►   TextGrid
                                                            │
                                                pronunciation dictionary
                                                     acoustic model
```

Two resources do the work, and the distinction matters for QC:

- The **pronunciation dictionary** maps each orthographic word to a phone sequence
  (`league → L IY1 G`). A word that is **not in the dictionary** has no pronunciation, so the
  aligner cannot align it. It is reported as **OOV** (out of vocabulary) and typically
  aligned as `spn` ("spoken noise") — a black box that can swallow a large stretch of speech.
  **OOVs are the number-one cause of local alignment damage, and they are the easiest to fix.**
- The **acoustic model** is a trained model of what those phones sound like. It is what makes
  the boundaries land where they do.

**Where those two come from.** MFA publishes pretrained acoustic models and pronunciation
dictionaries for many languages, catalogued at

- MFA models and dictionaries — [mfa-models.readthedocs.io](https://mfa-models.readthedocs.io/)
  `https://mfa-models.readthedocs.io/`

**Do not pick a pair off that page for lab work.** Which model and dictionary your project uses
is a project decision, not a preference — see §2.

**The output** is a TextGrid with (at least) a `words` tier and a `phones` tier. Phone labels
depend on the models you used: `english_us_arpa` emits **ARPABET with a stress digit on
vowels** (`AA1`, `IH0`, `EY2`), while `english_mfa` emits MFA's **IPA-based** symbols. **Silence
is not labelled at all** — MFA 3.x strips silence labels by default, so silences and pauses show
as **blank intervals on both tiers**. (There is no `sp` phone in MFA 3.x; if you meet one, you
are looking at output from an older version or another tool.) The one label that *is* always
there when it applies is **`spn`**, for a word the dictionary could not pronounce. Every phone nests inside its word; every word nests in the file. This is exactly the
structure the lab's own TextGrid reader expects.

**What alignment is not.** It is not correct by construction. It is a model's best fit of a
transcript to a signal, and when the transcript and the signal disagree — a mis-transcribed
word, an OOV, background noise, overlapping speech — the aligner does not stop. It produces
a confident, well-formed, wrong TextGrid.

## 2. The standard MFA workflow, step by step

This section teaches the **publicly documented MFA workflow**: the same five steps whatever
corpus you point it at. Two things are *not* here because they are project decisions rather
than tool knowledge — **which acoustic model and dictionary your project uses, and where its
aligned output belongs.** Ask the **Lab Manager or the PI** for those before you run anything
on lab data. Ask one more thing at the same time: **does this corpus already ship an alignment,
and what is its status?** For the corpus most students meet first, part of that is answered —
**the public-facing version of the lab's corpus has already been aligned with MFA.** That tells
you an alignment exists. It does **not** tell you the alignment is good: **to the lab's
knowledge it has not been QC'd.** That is not the same as knowing it is bad; it means **nobody
has recorded checking it**, so you treat it as material to check, not material to trust — which is the reason this module spends more pages on QC (§5) than on running the
aligner. It is also **not** the rejected alignment §5.3 describes; that was an earlier one. If
your work depends on the quality of a shipped alignment, ask the PI or the Lab Manager what is
known about it, and check it yourself regardless.

> **This module was written against MFA 3.4, and MFA's command line changes between releases.**
> The lab tracks the current release — **4.0 is expected later this year**, and the lab will
> upgrade when it lands, at which point some of the invocations below will need re-checking.
> **Run `mfa version` first and believe it over this page.** If it prints something other than
> 3.4, check each command against MFA's own documentation (§1) before running it on anything
> that matters. This is normal for this tool, not a sign that something is broken.
>
> The canonical worked example, and the reference this walkthrough follows, is MFA's own:
> [MFA alignment example](https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/en/latest/first_steps/alignment_example.html#alignment-example)
> `https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/en/latest/first_steps/alignment_example.html#alignment-example`

### Step 1 — Get MFA running

MFA is installed from the **conda-forge** channel. If you have neither `mamba` nor `conda` on
your machine yet, install **Miniforge** first — it is the conventional route and it gives you
both: [Miniforge downloads](https://conda-forge.org/download/) —
`https://conda-forge.org/download/`. *(On a shared lab machine, ask the Lab Manager or the PI
before installing anything.)*

```bash
mamba create -n aligner -c conda-forge montreal-forced-aligner
conda activate aligner
mfa version
```

**`mamba` and `conda` are interchangeable here** — mamba is a drop-in replacement and resolves
much faster, which is why it is used for the create step. MFA's own documentation writes both
lines with `conda`, so if you are following that page instead, you are not doing anything
different. **Use `conda activate`**: `mamba activate` needs shell initialisation that is often
missing and fails with an unhelpful message.

**Why not `pip` or `uv`?** MFA's aligner is not pure Python — it depends on compiled Kaldi
components that are not published as installable wheels for every platform, so a `pip`/`uv`
install gives you a package that imports and then cannot align (and on macOS would need a
source toolchain build). Install it the way above and skip that afternoon.

`mfa version` printing a version number is the whole test. Everything else in this module
assumes that `aligner` environment is active. Solving the environment is still the slowest
step of your first run — mamba is much faster at it than conda, but start it before you need
it rather than while a participant waits.

MFA keeps downloaded models, temporary files, and run logs in its own directory — by default
`~/Documents/MFA`. When a run fails, the log there is the first place to look.

### Step 2 — Get an acoustic model and a dictionary

Pretrained models and dictionaries are downloaded by name:

```bash
mfa model download acoustic english_mfa
mfa model download dictionary english_mfa
mfa model list acoustic
mfa model list dictionary
```

**`english_mfa` is the lab's default pair.** Use it unless you are told otherwise.

**The one standing exception: `english_us_arpa`.** Work that builds on **Labov, Rosenfelder &
Fruehwald (2013), "One hundred years of sound change in Philadelphia", *Language* 89(1), 30–65,
doi:10.1353/lan.2013.0015** —
the Philadelphia lineage the lab's sociolinguistic corpus work descends from — uses
`english_us_arpa` instead, so that the phone set and the measurement conventions match the
literature it is being compared against. If your project is in that lineage, you will be told;
if you are unsure, ask the PI or the Lab Manager before you download anything.

> **Dictionary, acoustic model and g2p model must share a phone alphabet — never mix alphabets.**
> The two families use **different ones**: `english_us_arpa` is **ARPABET** (`AA1`, `IH0`, `EY2` —
> stress digits on vowels), while `english_mfa` uses MFA's **IPA-based** set. A dictionary that
> emits symbols the acoustic model has never seen does not fail cleanly; it produces bad
> alignments or refuses phones one at a time.
>
> **The names are not symmetrical, and this catches people.** In the MFA family, **only the
> acoustic model is dialect-neutral** — plain `english_mfa`. **Dictionaries come both ways**
> (`english_mfa`, and dialect-split `english_us_mfa`, UK, India, Nigeria, Nonnative), and
> **G2P models are dialect-split only**: there is no `english_mfa` G2P at all.
>
> **So do not match on the name — match on the alphabet.** MFA's own alignment example, the page
> §2's walkthrough follows, shows exactly that, pairing a **dialect-split dictionary with the
> dialect-neutral acoustic model**:
>
> ```bash
> mfa model download acoustic english_mfa
> mfa model download dictionary english_us_mfa
> mfa align CORPUS english_us_mfa english_mfa OUTPUT --clean
> mfa model download g2p english_us_mfa
> ```
>
> Three different-looking names, one alphabet, and it is correct. That is the rule in one
> example: `english_mfa` and every `english_*_mfa` share MFA's IPA-based set, so they
> interoperate; `english_us_arpa` is ARPABET and does not mix with any of them.

The catalogue of what exists is the MFA models site linked in §1.

### Step 3 — Lay out the corpus directory

This is where most first runs fail, and it is entirely mechanical. MFA expects, in one
directory:

- an **audio file** (`.wav` is safest), and
- a **transcript file with exactly the same basename** — `.lab` for plain text, or a
  `.TextGrid` when you want to hand MFA your own utterance boundaries.

One subdirectory per speaker is the normal shape:

```
my_corpus/
├── speaker01/
│   ├── interview01.wav
│   └── interview01.lab
└── speaker02/
    ├── interview02.wav
    └── interview02.lab
```

**The basenames must match exactly.** `interview01.wav` with `interview1.lab` beside it is not
a pair — MFA sees audio with no transcript and skips it, and the run "succeeds" with nothing
aligned for that file.

### Step 4 — Validate before you align

```bash
mfa validate my_corpus/ english_mfa --acoustic_model_path english_mfa --output_directory validation_out/
```

Validation reads the corpus the way alignment will, without doing the alignment. It tells you
how many files it found, how many pairs it made, and — the part you are actually here for —
**which words are out of vocabulary.** Run it every time: it is far cheaper than the alignment
it protects, and it catches the failures that otherwise show up as a confident, wrong TextGrid
an hour later.

> **Two flags that are not optional in practice.**
> **`--acoustic_model_path` is an option, not a positional.** The dictionary is the only thing
> `mfa validate` takes positionally after the corpus. If you write the acoustic model as a third
> bare argument, MFA **silently discards it** and — with no model to test against — **trains a
> monophone model from your own corpus instead.** The command still "works": it just spends a
> long time doing something else and tells you nothing about the model you meant to check.
> **`--output_directory` is where the OOV files are written.** Without it you get counts on the
> console and no artifact to work from.

### Step 5 — Align, and find the output

```bash
mfa align my_corpus/ english_mfa english_mfa my_corpus_aligned/ --clean
```

The four positional arguments are **corpus in, dictionary, acoustic model, output directory** —
note that here the acoustic model *is* a positional, unlike `mfa validate` above. `--clean`
clears anything cached from a previous run of the same corpus. In MFA 3.x cleaning is already
the default; `--clean` states it explicitly, and `--no_clean` is the switch that changes
behaviour, when you deliberately want to reuse a previous run's work.

Output lands in `my_corpus_aligned/`, mirroring the input structure — one `.TextGrid` per input
audio file, each with a `words` tier and a `phones` tier. That file is what §4 teaches you to
read and §5 teaches you to distrust.

**Worth adding now rather than re-running later:** `--output_analysis` also writes
`alignment_analysis.csv` beside the TextGrids, which is the triage table §5.4 teaches. It is off
by default, and a corpus is slow to align twice.

## 3. Worked examples: the commands, and what they look like when they go wrong

Four steps do the work — download, validate, handle OOVs, align. For each: a realistic
invocation, what a good result looks like, and the failure you are most likely to hit.

### 3.1 `mfa model download` — fetching a model or dictionary

```bash
mfa model download acoustic english_mfa
```

**Good result:** it reports the model saved, and `mfa model list acoustic` then shows
`english_mfa` in the list.
**Most common failure:** no network, or a name that does not exist in the catalogue. A
misspelled name fails at download time, which is cheap — the same misspelling passed to
`mfa align` fails after the corpus has been read, which is not.

### 3.2 `mfa validate` — the step people skip

```bash
mfa validate my_corpus/ english_mfa --acoustic_model_path english_mfa --output_directory validation_out/
```

**Good result:** the file and speaker counts match what you put in the directory, and the
out-of-vocabulary list is empty or holds only words you expect (names, places, a coined word).
**Most common failures:** a file count lower than the number of recordings you have — which
means unpaired audio (Step 3's basename rule), not a broken corpus — and the silent one from
Step 4, **writing the acoustic model as a third positional**, which makes MFA train a model
from your corpus instead of checking the one you named.

### 3.3 Handling out-of-vocabulary words

An **OOV** is a word in your transcript with no pronunciation in the dictionary. The aligner
cannot place phone boundaries inside a word it cannot pronounce, so it brackets the region as
`spn` — the failure the practice fixtures illustrate, and the one worth being able to fix.
`practice-data/mfa/oov-report.txt` is an example of what an OOV list looks like.

There are three honest fixes, in order of preference:

1. **It is a typo.** Fix the transcript and re-validate. Most OOVs are this.
2. **It is a real word the dictionary lacks** (a name, a local place, a dialect form). Copy the
   downloaded dictionary — it lives under MFA's root, e.g.
   `~/Documents/MFA/pretrained_models/dictionary/english_mfa.dict` — and add a line to **your
   copy**: the word, then its phones, whitespace-separated. **Write the phones in the same
   alphabet the rest of that dictionary uses**, or the acoustic model will not know them. In an
   `english_us_arpa` dictionary that looks like

   ```
   iggles  IH1 G AH0 L Z
   ```

   and in an `english_mfa` dictionary it is the IPA-style symbols that file already uses. Then
   align against your copy by passing its **path** instead of the model name:

   ```bash
   mfa align my_corpus/ /path/to/my_dictionary.dict english_mfa my_corpus_aligned/
   ```
3. **There are too many to hand-write.** MFA ships grapheme-to-phoneme models that guess
   pronunciations from spelling:

   ```bash
   mfa model download g2p english_us_mfa
   mfa g2p oovs.txt english_us_mfa new_prons.txt
   ```

   **Note the name.** The G2P models are dialect-split, so there is no `english_mfa` G2P —
   `english_us_mfa` is the US-English one, and it shares the `english_mfa` pair's IPA-based
   alphabet (§2 Step 2). For the ARPABET family the equivalent is `english_us_arpa`.

   Then append `new_prons.txt` to your dictionary copy. **Read what it produced before you use
   it** — a G2P guess is a guess, and a wrong pronunciation aligns silently.

**Never edit the shared downloaded dictionary in place.** Work on a copy, so the next person's
run is not quietly different from yours.

### 3.4 `mfa align` — the run itself

```bash
mfa align my_corpus/ english_mfa english_mfa my_corpus_aligned/ --clean
```

**Good result:** every input file has a `.TextGrid` beside it in the output directory, and the
run reports no unaligned files. Then you QC it (§5) — a completed run is not a good alignment.
**Most common failures:**

- **"Unaligned" or skipped files.** Usually one utterance the decoder could not fit — often an
  enormous single utterance (see §6.1) or audio that does not match its transcript at all.
- **A run that takes far longer than expected.** Same cause, usually: one very long utterance
  with no internal boundaries.
- **An empty output directory after a "successful" run.** Nothing was paired. Go back to Step 3.

## 4. Reading the output

Open `practice-data/mfa/practice-aligned.TextGrid` — in Praat if you have it (Module 2 §1),
or with the shipped viewer if you do not:

```bash
cd practice-data/mfa
python3 inspect_textgrid.py practice-aligned.TextGrid
python3 inspect_textgrid.py practice-aligned.TextGrid --tier phones --min-dur 0.3
```

The viewer prints intervals and durations and **makes no judgement** — it sorts the haystack;
finding the needle is your job.

*(The practice TextGrid uses **ARPABET** phone labels, as `english_us_arpa` emits, because it
models the corpus work in that lineage (§2 Step 2). Your own `english_mfa` output will carry
IPA-style symbols instead. Same structure, different alphabet — every check below works on
either.)*

The TextGrid format itself — tiers, intervals, and what an aligner writes into them — is
described in MFA's documentation:
[montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io](https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/) —
`https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/`.

What to look at first, every time:

- **Do the tiers span the same time?** Both should start at 0 and end at the file's end.
  **Blank intervals are normal** — that is what silence looks like in MFA 3.x output, on both
  tiers.
- **Do the words on the words tier match the transcript?** If the aligner stopped partway,
  the tail of the transcript simply is not there.
- **Is there an OOV report, and is it empty?** A non-empty OOV list tells you where to look
  before you look anywhere else.
- **Sort the phones by duration.** This one habit finds most gross failures in seconds.

## 5. QC: spotting misalignment, and re-run vs. hand-correct

### 5.1 What misalignment looks like without audio

You cannot always listen. These signatures are visible in the TextGrid alone:

| Signature | Usual cause |
|---|---|
| A **single very long phone**, especially `spn` | An **OOV word** the aligner could not pronounce; it absorbs the surrounding speech |
| A **stressed vowel far longer than any plausible vowel** | The aligner lost the thread and over-assigned time |
| A **word crushed to near-zero duration** | The compensating half of the failure above |
| **Words missing** relative to the transcript | Alignment stopped partway, or the transcript and audio are misaligned wholesale |
| **Very few words per minute** across a whole file | A flawed or incomplete alignment run — see §5.3 |

**The compensation principle, and it is the thing to actually learn.** A forced aligner is
fitting a *fixed* sequence of phones to a *fixed* stretch of time. Time it wrongly gives to
one segment must come out of a neighbour. **So misalignments come in adjacent compensating
pairs — an implausibly long interval next to an implausibly short one.** When you find one,
look immediately on both sides. Two symptoms are usually one error, and reporting them as two
independent problems means you have not understood the failure.

### 5.2 Re-run or hand-correct?

**Default: re-run.** Fix the *cause* — add the word to the dictionary, correct the
transcript, re-segment the utterance — and align again.

**Hand-correcting boundaries is a last resort**, and it carries a real cost: a hand-edited
TextGrid is no longer reproducible from its inputs, so nobody downstream can regenerate it.
It also runs straight into a standing lab rule — **never hand-edit a machine-written file**:
if a script wrote it, a script corrects it. If you ever do hand-correct, that
is a **documented, logged decision** with the PI, not a quiet fix.

**Exclusion is a legitimate third option.** A stretch that will not align cleanly can be
excluded — with a logged, criterion-based reason, exactly as for formant tokens (Module 2 §4).

### 5.3 A cautionary case from the lab's own corpus work

A corpus the lab works with arrived with an `MFA_Aligned_TextGrids/` directory already in it.
Someone checked it instead of using it. **It was rejected, and the corpus was re-aligned from
the verbatim transcripts.** The diagnosis, confirmed across all 38 speakers, was that the
pre-existing words tier carried only **1.8 to 8.2 words per minute** — an order of magnitude
below conversational speech — and **stopped partway through each interview**. Two worked cases,
*with illustrative figures invented for this module — the real per-speaker numbers stay in the
project's own records*: over the stretch each one did cover, **speaker A's verbatim transcript
carried about 1,450 words where the shipped tier had about 760; speaker B's about 1,900 against
about 880.** Roughly **half the words missing even where the alignment ran** — and because it
also **stopped partway through the file**, the rate measured across the whole recording collapsed
into the band above. **Those two counts illustrate the deficit ratio, not the rate:** to get a
words-per-minute figure from them you would need each file's own duration, and the two shortest
files in the set are what put the bottom of the band at 1.8. Two symptoms, one failed run; that
is exactly the pair Exercise 3 teaches you to report together, and the reason Exercise 3 asks for
both. Re-aligning was not a marginal improvement.

Two more lessons from the same work, both worth having in advance:

- **Segmentation matters.** A first attempt flattened each speaker's ~4,000-word transcript
  into a single utterance against a 60–90 minute file. *(That is one speaker's own words in an
  interview that also contains the interviewer and long pauses — not the same quantity as
  Exercise 3's words-per-minute, which divides **all** aligned words by **total** file duration.
  Do not compare the two numbers directly.)* It **failed outright for 3 of 38
  speakers**, each stalling the decoder for 80–108 seconds with no output. Re-using the
  transcript's own per-interval boundaries fixed it: a bad stretch then costs one interval
  rather than an entire interview.
- **Transcription conventions must be stripped before alignment.** ELAN markup (cut-offs,
  emphasis, punctuation) is not in the dictionary, so leaving it in **inflates the OOV rate**
  and damages alignment. That project strips it by regex and passes interval timings through
  untouched.

**The transferable habit: never assume a shipped alignment is good. Measure its coverage
before you build on it.** That is Exercise 3.

### 5.4 Reading MFA's alignment analysis

§5.1's signatures are read **inside one file**; Exercise 3's words-per-minute and coverage
checks are read **across files**. MFA can also give you a third view: an **alignment-analysis
table**, written alongside the aligned TextGrids, with **one row per utterance**. Where the
first two views tell you *what* went wrong and *where*, this one tells you **which files to
look at first** — before you have opened a single TextGrid.

**Run the alignment, then read this table before you trust a single measurement downstream.**

**Ask for it explicitly.** MFA's documentation says the file appears automatically when
TextGrids are exported, but in the 3.4 code the write is gated behind a setting,
**`output_analysis`, which is off by default** — so on a default run there is nothing to read.
Turn it on: set **`output_analysis: true`** in the configuration YAML you pass to the run, or add
**`--output_analysis`** to the align command, which MFA accepts because it maps onto that same
setting.

```bash
mfa align my_corpus/ english_mfa english_mfa my_corpus_aligned/ --clean --output_analysis
```

**`alignment_analysis.csv`** then lands in the output directory beside the TextGrids. If it is
not there after a run, do not assume you misread the table — **ask the Lab Manager or the PI how
the lab produces it**, because this is a spot where MFA's documentation and its code disagree and
the answer may differ with your version.
[MFA — alignment analysis](https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user_guide/implementations/alignment_analysis.html)
`https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user_guide/implementations/alignment_analysis.html`

MFA 3.4 writes **ten columns, in this order**:

| # | Column | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `file` | The recording the utterance came from |
| 2–3 | `begin`, `end` | The utterance's span in seconds |
| 4 | `speaker` | Which speaker MFA attributed the utterance to — check this matches your corpus layout (§2 Step 3) |
| 5 | `overall_log_likelihood` | The model's average log-likelihood over the utterance. **Do not rank files by it** — see the warning below |
| 6 | `speech_log_likelihood` | The same, but **excluding silence**: the average of per-phone log-likelihoods over speech only. Same warning applies |
| 7 | `phone_duration_deviation` | The **maximum absolute z-score of any phone's log-duration** in the utterance, z-scored against that phone's mean and SD **across the whole corpus**. Higher = something in this utterance is durationally strange |
| 8 | `max_running_short_interval` | The longest run of consecutive intervals of **10 ms or less**. A long run means part of the transcript was not aligned, or got shoved together at the start or end of the file |
| 9 | `snr` | Mean intensity of the **speech** phones divided by mean intensity of the **silence** in that utterance |
| 10 | `intensity_deviation` | How far the utterance's intensity departs from the corpus norm |

**Note where `max_running_short_interval` sits** — between the duration deviation and `snr`, not
at the end. A file produced by another version, or an extract someone made earlier, may carry
fewer columns or a different order; **read the header of the file in front of you** rather than
counting positions from memory. (The practice fixture for Exercise 5 carries columns 1–7 and
`snr`, which is enough for the triage the exercise teaches.)

> ⚠ **The one that catches everybody: log-likelihood is not comparable across speakers.** It
> looks like the obvious quality number, and sorting by it is the wrong move. MFA aligns in two
> passes and estimates **per-speaker feature transforms** along the way; how much those help
> depends on how much data a speaker has and how variable it is. Log-likelihood also absorbs
> mismatch between the model's training data and yours — language variety, speaking style,
> recording conditions. So a low value can mean a bad alignment, or a quiet speaker with three
> minutes of audio, or an accent the model was not trained on. **Ranking files by
> `overall_log_likelihood` or `speech_log_likelihood` to find bad alignments is a mistake.** Use
> the duration deviation for that.

**Three patterns to look for, in this order.**

1. **Rows whose metric columns are empty.** The analysis produced nothing for that utterance:
   the alignment did not happen, or did not survive. This is the loudest signal in the table and
   the easiest to miss, because an empty cell looks like nothing at all. **Those files get
   investigated. They never get silently dropped from a dataset** — a file that quietly
   disappears between the corpus and the analysis is a hole in your sample that nobody can see
   later.
2. **The top of the `phone_duration_deviation` ranking.** Because it is the *maximum* absolute
   z-score over the utterance's phones — not the mean — a single badly-placed boundary shows up
   even in an otherwise clean utterance. That is deliberate: over-long and over-short phones come
   in compensating pairs (§5.1), and averaging would let them cancel to nothing. Durations are
   z-scored in the **log** domain because durations are positive and right-skewed, so a raw
   z-score would flag every long phone.
   **Treat this column as a queue, not a verdict.** MFA's own documentation says false positives
   are expected here, and that this is the intended trade — a false positive costs you a look, a
   false negative costs you a wrong number in a paper. **Sort descending, work down the list, and
   stop when the files you open stop being real problems.** The absolute value matters much less
   than the rank.
3. **Low `snr`.** Read it as *speech intensity over silence intensity in this utterance*, which
   has two consequences. First, if **no silence was aligned**, MFA substitutes the minimum
   intensity, so the number may be unreliable rather than informative. Second — and this is the
   useful part — **a word missing from the transcript gets aligned as silence**, which drags SNR
   down. So **low SNR together with a high duration deviation points at a transcript problem**,
   not merely a noisy room; low SNR with ordinary deviations usually is just the room. Either
   way you cannot re-align your way out of a genuinely bad recording — that becomes a logged
   decision about whether the file belongs in the study (§5.2).

**How this fits with the other two views.** The analysis table is a **triage instrument** — it
ranks files by how much they need your attention. The words-per-minute and coverage checks
(Exercise 3) confirm *whether a file's alignment actually covers the speech*, and §5.1's
signatures diagnose *what went wrong inside it*. Use them in that order and you spend your
attention where it pays: table first, coverage second, TextGrid last.

**One thing this table cannot do:** tell you an alignment is *right*. Healthy-looking numbers
mean nothing flagged itself — not that boundaries are where they belong. MFA's documentation
notes that these measures are still being refined, which is another way of saying the same
thing: they are instruments for directing suspicion, not proof of correctness. Measuring
accuracy is a different procedure — §5.5.

### 5.5 Measuring accuracy: evaluating against a gold standard

Everything above **flags suspicion**. Only one procedure in this module **measures accuracy**,
and it is the one a lab reaches for when it needs a defensible number about a corpus — for
instance a corpus whose shipped alignment has not, to the lab's knowledge, been QC'd (§2).

**The idea.** You hand-correct a **sample** of utterances until their boundaries are right —
that sample is your **gold standard** — and then have MFA compare its own output against it.

```bash
mfa align my_corpus/ english_mfa english_mfa my_corpus_aligned/ --reference_directory gold/
```

`--reference_directory` points at TextGrids in the shape MFA itself exports (phone and speaker
tiers per speaker). If your gold standard came from somewhere with a **different phone set**,
`--custom_mapping_path` takes a YAML file that reconciles the two.

**Get the direction right, because it fails silently.** **The key is the phone your acoustic
model outputs; the values are the reference symbols that should count as matching it** — model
on the left, gold standard on the right. Use a list when several reference symbols are
acceptable for one model phone:

```yaml
AA0: aa
N: [en, n]
```

Read that as: where MFA outputs `AA0`, a reference `aa` is a match; where MFA outputs `N`, both
`en` and `n` in the reference count as matches. **Write it the other way round and the keys match
no model phone at all** — nothing is exempted, every mapped phone scores as a substitution, and
because substitutions count double in the formula below, your error rate inflates while MFA
reports a perfectly confident number.

**What you get back**, per utterance:

- **Overlap score** — **not** a coverage percentage, and worth knowing before it confuses you.
  MFA builds it from a cost, `-1 × (|begin_aligned − begin_ref| + |end_aligned − end_ref| +
  0 if the labels match, 2 if they do not)`, and reports **that cost divided by 2**, averaged
  over the utterance's phones (excluding phones aligned to silence, and insertions and
  deletions). So it is **always ≤ 0**, it is denominated in **seconds** plus that label penalty,
  and there is no 0–1 scale: **closer to zero is better**, and a value like −0.06 means the
  boundaries sit about that far from the reference on average, not "6 % of something".
- **Phone error rate** — **(insertions + deletions + 2 × substitutions) ÷ length of the
  reference**. Substitutions count double because a substitution is both a wrong phone placed
  and a right phone missed.

**When it is warranted.** Not in week 1, and not on every corpus. It is worth the hand-correction
cost when a result depends on boundary accuracy, when you inherit an alignment nobody has
checked, or when you need to state an error rate in a methods section rather than assert that
the alignment "looked fine". Ask the PI or the Lab Manager before committing time to it — the
sample has to be big enough to mean something and hand-correcting is slow.
[MFA — alignment evaluation](https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user_guide/implementations/alignment_evaluation.html)
`https://montreal-forced-aligner.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user_guide/implementations/alignment_evaluation.html`

## 6. Applications to LUV Lab data

### 6.1 What this lab's recordings do to the workflow

The corpora this lab works with are **sociolinguistic interviews**: one long recording, an
interviewer and a speaker talking naturally, an hour or more at a time. Every step in §2 still
applies — but four properties of that material decide whether a run succeeds.

**They are long.** A 60–90 minute file handed to the aligner as *one utterance* is the single
most reliable way to make a run fail or crawl: the decoder has to fit thousands of words to an
hour of audio in one pass, with no place to recover if it drifts. **Give the aligner the
transcript's own interval boundaries** — hand it a `.TextGrid` with utterance intervals rather
than one flat `.lab`. Then a stretch that will not align costs you that interval, not the
interview.

**They have more than one voice in them.** The interviewer is on the recording too. Whatever
scheme your project uses to separate speakers — separate tiers, separate files, a speaker
subdirectory per §2 Step 3 — apply it *before* aligning, and know which speaker each aligned
TextGrid actually describes. A vowel measured from the interviewer's speech is not a data point
about the participant.

**They are disfluent, and transcribed as such.** Natural speech carries false starts,
cut-offs, laughter, overlap. Transcription conventions encode those with markup — and **markup
is not in the pronunciation dictionary**, so every convention symbol left in the transcript
becomes an OOV, and OOVs become `spn` boxes that swallow real speech (§3.3, §5.1). **Strip
transcription markup before alignment and pass the interval timings through untouched.** Do
not "clean" the transcript by rewriting what the speaker said; strip the notation, keep the
words.

**They contain names, places, and community vocabulary.** Expect a genuine OOV list — not
every OOV is an error. Fix the typos, add the real words to your own dictionary copy (§3.3),
and keep a note of what you added: the next person aligning the same corpus needs the same
additions, or their run differs from yours for no visible reason.

**They vary in recording quality.** Interviews are recorded in people's homes and in public
places, not in the booth. That is a strength of the data and a nuisance for the aligner, and it
is exactly what the `snr` column of the alignment analysis (§5.4) makes visible. **On a corpus
this size, read the analysis table first and let it tell you which recordings to open** — the
alternative is opening sixty TextGrids in file-name order and running out of afternoon.

**And the point of all of it:** the lab's downstream measurements are made *inside* the
intervals this workflow produces. That is §6.2.

### 6.2 How aligned intervals feed vowel extraction — the link to Module 2

This is where the two modules join, and it is the whole reason you learned both.

1. The aligner produces phone intervals: `AA1` from 4.950 s to 5.660 s. **That is deliberately
   the bad interval from Exercise 2 (D2)** — follow it through the next three steps and you can
   see exactly what an alignment error costs downstream.
2. An extraction script walks the phones tier, keeps intervals whose label is a vowel with
   the wanted stress, and records the surrounding phones as context. The repo's worked
   example is a `iter_vowel_tokens(...)` helper in the lab's own TextGrid reader, which yields
   exactly that — vowel, stress, word, `tmin`, `tmax`, preceding and following phone.
3. For each token, a formant measurement is taken **inside that interval** — trimmed away
   from the edges to avoid consonantal transitions, then summarized (Module 2 §2–3;
   `lib/formants.py`).
4. Those measurements are normalized and modelled.

**So a bad boundary is a bad formant, silently.** If the interval labelled `AA1` actually
covers the preceding /w/ and half the vowel, the tracker measures that faithfully and returns
a number that is wrong in a way no downstream QC catches. This is why the duration filters in
Module 2 §3.3 exist: **a wildly long or wildly short vowel is usually an alignment failure
wearing a formant's clothing.** They are the seam between the two modules, and they are your
cheapest detector.
